## Abstract

Inheritance law in the Brazilian legal system faces a series of problems regarding the administration of family assets. The greatest of them occurs in the probate (inventory) process, since many family businesses tend to lose all of the decedent’s assets (the *de cujus*) for lack of succession and tax planning for their administration. This reality is most evident in agribusiness companies, in which family management is very common. It is therefore necessary to organize the administration of the assets, with a view to their transfer after death and to the survival of the family business without major losses during the succession. In this field, the family holding company presents itself as a possible solution: a way of administering the family’s common assets, in matters of succession and business management, without interference from the subjective interests of the members of the company.

**Keywords:** Law of succession; Family succession; Administrative and tax planning; Family holding companies.

## Introduction

The institution of family succession is extremely present in the Brazilian legal system, because it is part of a moment in the life of every human being: all citizens, regardless of gender, color or social class, are born, grow, build assets and die.

Depending on the assets that were built, a need arises to prepare successors for the possible administration of the assets left by the deceased, notably when those assets consist of a family business.

This reality stands out mainly in rural areas, present in most Brazilian states, where small and large producers began, or still today keep, their assets administered by the family.

However, many of these family businesses do not endure over the years, since all human beings, regardless of choice, are bound to die, whatever the cause.

It is therefore essential that there be a certain organization in the administration of this family property, through succession planning whose purpose is to keep the company going after the death of its family leader. And this is where the possible creation of holding companies comes in.

In this sense, the problem addressed by this work arises: how can this planning be carried out, both in the succession field and in the tax aspect, to ensure the survival of family businesses through succession? Can the creation of a holding company help in this planning, and in managing the assets and the conflicts of interest among heirs, to ensure the survival of the company?

Accordingly, the purpose of this article is to analyze the creation of family holding companies for the administration of assets, notably of family businesses, as a possible solution to the conflicts caused by the influence of the subjective interests of family members, in general but mainly within agribusiness, in order to avoid the dissipation of assets.

Based on this problem and this objective, and following a deductive, systemic and axiological method, the article presents considerations on family succession and its optimization, as well as on planning that succession in the administrative and tax fields.

Afterwards, it addresses the creation of family holding companies as a possible solution to the problem, facilitating planning and the management of conflicts of interest among heirs.

## 1. Family Succession

Brazilian succession law derives, like family law, from various social transformations faced around the world over the centuries. These changes reflect directly on the way the family is organized, which was once predominantly patriarchal: the father was the only person responsible for the offspring and also the only one with the capacity to perform acts of civil life.

Today, family organization takes a different form, since all members of the family are seen by the State as equals, with no distinction between the patriarch, his wife and his children.

When one speaks of family law and its constitution, succession law appears, since it is this institution that regulates the administration of the family property from the moment of the death of the *de cujus*, that is, the deceased whose assets are in probate.

On this subject, Diniz (2014, v. 6, p. 25) teaches that “the idea of succession revolves around the permanence of a legal relationship, which subsists despite the change of its respective holders.”

There are two forms of succession: *inter vivos* and *causa mortis*. The first conveys the idea of the way in which one person succeeds another, holding in whole or in part the rights that belonged to that person, as, for example, the buyer who succeeds the debtor in a purchase and sale relationship.

The second form, also known as succession in the strict sense, is the total or partial transmission of the estate upon the death of the holder of rights, who transmits them to one or more heirs.

Succession in the strict sense, according to the doctrinal understanding of Lisboa (2013, v. 5, p. 339), is “the replacement of a natural person or legal entity by another, who assumes all the rights and obligations of the one replaced or succeeded, through the existing modes of acquisition.”

Therefore, from these understandings, it follows that for succession in the strict sense to exist, certain requirements must be met, namely:

- a) The actual death of the holder of the estate;
- b) If the death is not known, a declaration of the holder’s absence, which must be made through the courts;
- c) The legitimacy of the heirs or legatees to receive the asset.

In addition to these requirements, the different forms of succession must also be taken into account. Among them stand out testamentary succession, which derives from an effective will representing the last wishes of the *de cujus*; and legitimate (intestate) succession, which results from the law and occurs in case of absence, nullity, voidability or lapse of the will.

From then on, the probate (inventory) of the *de cujus*’s assets begins. It is the procedure for legalizing the distribution of the estate left by the deceased and, in general terms, it must be carried out under the supervision of the Judiciary through a proceeding of its own.

This procedure is the most widely used means of dividing the family property among the heirs, a fact that, besides being costly, ends up being wearing for the family, since during the process conflicts of subjective interests commonly arise among the heirs, which often have nothing to do with the main focus: the administration of the property they hold in common.

The result of these conflicts of interest in succession is, in most cases, the collapse of family businesses, bringing about the well-known popular saying “rich father, noble son, poor grandson.” This is because family disputes harm the administration of the company as well as the distribution of assets, since, because of the existing conflicts, it is not known who will manage sector “x” or who will keep asset “y” left by the author of the estate.

## 2. Optimized Succession

Planning an effective succession is the first step for a company to survive a conflictual succession. For success, without major losses, certain elements must be applied; otherwise, the family business will be doomed to failure, being no more than one more frustrated attempt at a failed dream.

It is important to stress that this problem is even more acute in agribusiness, which in Brazil is made up of small and large farmers who invest in having family members on their staff, or who started their businesses with family members as collaborators, but who, for lack of planning, end up stagnating their growth, with business failure as the result.

Many companies develop their future leaders according to the needs of the current business, not of the future business, and this is a big mistake. The profile of skills, values, personality and experience of any successor should fit the place where the family business is going, not the point where it is today.

> Most companies and their fields of activity are changing at a rapid pace, aren’t they? It is not hard to imagine that a company started today will, within a generation, transform into a different set of products and services, is it? If that happens, it will need to be managed in a very different way, even if it maintains its values and ethics, and it will require a successor with a profile different from that of the current leader. (DAVIS, 2016, p. 7-8)

It is well known that when the right questions are asked, the right answers follow. Therefore, one asks: how can an excellent succession be planned? Or, what can the family members, together, do to produce effective management, thereby optimizing the succession?

According to studies carried out over time in the Families Business program of Harvard Business School (DAVIS, 2016), certain requirements must be observed when planning succession. These requirements are analyzed from the profile of the successor.

The research carried out by Davis (2016, p. 16-22) led to seven points that must be observed.

The first concerns earning the “respect of managers, employees, board members, external stakeholders and family. He or she was the undisputed choice to take over. The family is at peace with this choice” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 16).

Second, Davis (2016, p. 16) highlights that the successor must have “the necessary external experience and business training to be prepared to lead the family company, given its present and future issues.”

> He or she is prepared for the specific leadership role, which fits their greatest and best use. External business experience is an important ingredient in preparing successors because, for most companies, the business is changing rapidly and we can learn a lot from how others do business successfully. Useful external experience can be obtained in a sector related to, or very different from, that of the family company.
>
> Education in management and other topics (technology is a common one) is also valuable. To be an effective business leader today, continuing education is necessary to stay up to date. Executive education does not necessarily mean obtaining an MBA, although, at the right time of life, such an academic experience can be interesting, teaching useful frameworks for analyzing business situations and broadening the successor’s skills and understanding of the business world. An MBA and other educational experiences can help a great deal if the successor is also motivated to learn and go further, with good sense and humility. Without these characteristics, an MBA can even be an impediment, because it may give the leader a false sense of confidence. (DAVIS, 2016, p. 16)

Another point to observe is that, besides external experience, the successor also needs to have internal experience in the business, knowing how the company works. Indeed, “one of the greatest myths perpetuated by business schools over the last 30 years is that an MBA allows one to manage well virtually any company” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 17).

In this sense, Davis (2016, p. 17-18) highlights:

> The truth is that without a visceral understanding of your business, you will not be able to manage it well. We teach successors to stay very close to what we call “The Street”: what employees do, how they relate to the company; who the customers and suppliers are and how loyal they are to the company; how money is made in it, and so on.
>
> To obtain the necessary internal experience, the successor needs to join the family company early enough to move through the appropriate functions and acquire the necessary experience. Some leaders believe that successors should learn about the family company from a very early age, starting with low-level jobs, as generally happens with other professionals. This may or may not be a good strategy. It makes sense if the goal is to develop the successor and make him or her a great operator of the family business and if there is the impression that the family company will not change much; in that condition, he or she should go through a series of functions starting from the bottom of the hierarchy. In that case, it is also advisable to join the family business earlier, but still after obtaining useful external experience.
>
> The family company will probably go through many changes and may transform into something very different. In that case, a later entry and a broader preparation program are justified. For example, if you think you need a successor who understands how to make the family company grow through acquisitions, you should encourage him or her to develop deep experience in investment banking before returning to the family company.
>
> In any case, I would say that knowledge of the “Street” of the business is necessary, but not sufficient, in the highly changeable business world in which we live. A business leader today also needs to understand what is happening in the sectors of the family company, and so on. Keeping one’s feet on the “Street” while gaining enough altitude to see what is happening around the company requires some stretching. The successor’s development program needs to incorporate both elements.

The fourth point deals with passion for the business. In fact, the person who will succeed must necessarily be passionate about the company’s activity. “The best-managed companies are always led by people who are passionate about what they do. They like the company’s products and services. They like the employees, the suppliers and the other people with whom the company does business” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 19).

On this element, Davis (2016, p. 19) highlights:

> They (mainly) love the hours and the daily routine. They even love the problems. They could not think of a company they would rather be in. When a succession candidate talks about the business and his or her eyes do not light up, he or she is not a good option.
>
> But do not jump to hasty conclusions about the successor’s suitability. Sometimes a successor has no enthusiasm for the business the parent started but is passionate about the version he or she wants to create. The question is whether this new approach makes sense for the family. Or the successor may actually like the current business but not be excited about being an operating CEO, as the parent was. Instead, he or she would like to manage the diversification of the family company at the level of a holding company. The question is whether it is useful to diversify the company and whether the holding’s role really adds value. Successful succession is always a dance between what the company needs and what the successor is good at and passionate about. Be careful not to leave a good business or create a new company just because the successor wants to. It is necessary to weigh carefully how to use his or her talents and interests well. There is always a compatible solution to these puzzles.

Fifth, “the successor must have, or be able to obtain through alliances, governance and other means, control of ownership. This means that the business leader can often count on the owners supporting his or her initiatives” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 20).

This is because, in the company’s activities, when something bolder must be done, “the owners will be inclined to support the CEO in the move (generally with an additional endorsement from the board). Sometimes successors have complete control of ownership” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 20). However, this is often difficult to achieve, especially in the case of a family company where the family is relatively large or there are few assets to be transferred.

> I do not want to sound Machiavellian when speaking about the leader protecting power in the family business system. And I do not advocate that business leaders obtain owners’ support through trading favors, building coalitions that exclude some owners from discussions, or unnecessary secrecy. These political methods are seen as disrespectful by some, often bounce back on the leader, and prevent building broad ownership unity and support for the family company.
>
> Successors need to learn how to constructively develop owners’ support for their leadership, as well as their commitment to the company. The focus on successor development concerns his or her management and leadership skills. Ownership skills are often almost as important for the successor’s future success. Successors in family companies need to be developed as leaders of owner groups, who know what the company is and how it should be legally organized, what the ownership is and should be structured like, what the owners’ responsibilities and rights are, and how to design effective governance of the owner group. A diplomatic style, the willingness to communicate and ask others for their support, and a measure of humility also help a great deal. (DAVIS, 2016, p. 20-21)

Moreover, “the successor needs to have his or her own loyal team in action at the top of the company. Developing loyalty to a new leader (and not only to the family company) is important for the leadership team to function well” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 21).

Indeed, this team must work together and with loyalty to the leader, as in a soccer team, in which each one performs his or her role well, cooperating with the others and remaining loyal to the leader.

> The current leader’s team tends to be personally loyal to him or her, and that condition is not always easily transferred to the next one. The successor can be patient and wait for senior managers to retire. It is common for most of them to be close in age to the outgoing leader, so they tend to retire a few years later. Some of these departures can be accelerated to ensure that the new senior management team has the skill and approach the successor wants. Complete changes in senior management, however, tend to be too revolutionary for companies.
>
> So it is inevitable that the transfer of loyalty requires the incoming successor to win over managers and employees before the succession takes place. It is essential that the outgoing leader explicitly endorse his or her successor and encourage senior management and other key employees to trust the heir’s judgment and character. (DAVIS, 2016, p. 21-22)

Finally, the last point highlighted is that the successor must have a real desire to lead. In fact, he or she may present all of the previous elements. But without the spirit and will to lead, one will get nowhere. Indeed, “leaders are largely responsible for finding out where to go and helping to get to the destination. The most successful successors I have studied are change agents” (DAVIS, 2016, p. 22).

Davis (2016, p. 22) highlights that, for the successor to perform well in this, he or she must always state a position on issues, but must also accept the opinions and criticism of those who disagree. “Almost every difficult decision involves disappointing someone, and few people feel comfortable deciding complicated matters by stating their positions or experiencing the disappointment or anger of others.”

It is also important to note that, as observed by Davis (2016, p. 23), “whenever the new generation is developed to produce strong future business leaders, good business owners and mature, united family members, the family creates an organization that almost no one can beat.”

Therefore, when successors are observed as well as trained, the result is an optimized succession, or a successful succession, which is a fundamental step toward the endurance of the family business over the years.

## 3. Succession Planning for the Family Business

Having understood the above, it follows that, for the reality of conflicts of interest among the successors of the family business to be eliminated from the Brazilian legal system, these businesses need so-called succession planning. This is the institution that regulates the destination of the company’s administration, whether family-owned or not, and its subsistence over time, investing in the training of its current and future administrators for better business management.

Through this planning, the probability of the family business collapsing, which would occur in the division of the assets of the author of the estate within the probate process, is greatly reduced, given that the entire administration of the family property is destined for a future administration that was planned. In this way, the family business will not suffer major impacts when a third party interested in managing the asset enters.

> For some, succession is a process of transferring power that takes one to two years of important changes at the top of the company. In reality, succession begins many years earlier, when the children are still small. Along this long road, which separates childhood from the moment of transfer, many measures must be taken. We can list three groups of measures that precede the succession phase proper:
>
> a) Building the base of the successors.
>
> b) Their development plan.
>
> c) Organizational and legal measures. (LODI, 1987, p. 9)

Following this line of understanding, the idea is consolidated that planning the future administration of the family business begins very early, still in the childhood of the future successors.

Along these lines, this training begins with the education of the future successors, which reveals their vocation as well as the profile of each one, allowing the current administrator of the family business to assess the profile of his or her successors right away.

> In this transmission of the family’s vocation, the children also acquire a methodology of life: they learn to manage the great moments and the transition phases; respect for people, devotion to study, the cultivation of teachers, great friendships, the commitment of love, the attitude toward interpersonal conflicts, the value of work, respect for democratic values, love of country, the commitment to truth. Together with family influence and a view of the world, a good university education is very important. The great university places us before human discoveries and develops our capacity to think and feel, gives us colleagues who will be lifelong companions, privileges us with some great teachers, in short, introduces us to history.
>
> In this first phase of the successor’s formation, it is also important to highlight the experience of working outside the family company for a period of three to five years. Working outside helps the young person find his or her identity, that is, his or her worth without the protection of the family name, and find a repertoire of experiences that will have comparative value in the future for the decisions of his or her company. Working outside is useful so that learning mistakes are made away from the company and do not harm professional image. The children should work outside until they acquire a personality that enables them to enter the family company as truly mature professionals. (LODI, 1987, p. 10)

In this order of ideas, the succession planning of the family business is directly linked to the training and professionalization of successors for the optimized administration of the family property.

## 4. Tax Planning

Within succession planning and the training of the successors who will manage the family business, there is the figure of tax planning, which is intrinsic to these institutions. This is because it is through this planning that business improvement and greater profitability for the entrepreneur are achieved, since it reduces the tax burden on the company without resorting to so-called tax evasion.

> Tax collection is very important for any capitalist country. The organization of the State depends on resources, because all the actions carried out by the Government are financed by the volume of money obtained through taxes. However, when the proceeds of this tax collection are diverted to purposes different from those expected by the population, the citizen’s outrage is just and complaint is inevitable. Despite this, unfortunately, it is not legally possible to avoid paying taxes. But it is possible to delay payment, or even avoid it, without such an attitude becoming a crime. This phenomenon is called Tax Planning. That is, tax planning means exactly coordinating actions to avoid or delay the payment of taxes, without committing crimes against this tax “order.”
>
> Thus, there is no alternative for companies and citizens but to try to understand the existing forms of taxation (the tax regimes) and, from there, undertake efforts and actions to try to save money on tax payments. (BARBOZA, 2017, p. 11)

Regarding this planning, the effective participation of an accounting organization is undeniable, since the accountant is the professional responsible for assessing the risks and profits that each company will have through the actions to be implemented.

Therefore, within the idea of tax planning, accounting is a fundamental piece and one of the most important, since it is with accounting that planning is perfected, by applying its own principles and technical standards for the “determination of costs and preparation of financial statements, besides, of course, supporting the Tax Authorities in forming the data sets that feed reports and returns, which actually serve to fulfill the company’s ancillary tax obligations” (BARBOZA, 2017, p. 11).

However, for this planning to be optimized, it is essential that the accountant have knowledge of the current tax legislation, since if it is not observed there will be a series of negative implications for the entrepreneur, given that such rules are vast and wide-ranging, and it is up to the accountant to follow their details, nuances and changes.

> Accounting legislation is broad, as it is embodied in three levels of government (municipal, state and federal). And to make matters worse, the budget adjustments of these spheres of government end up producing an absurdly large number of changes in tax laws, because, each year, the country’s tax legislative framework changes in an immeasurable way, such that the accounting professional is directly affected. The diligent accountant follows the legislative changes at the three levels of government regarding the rules that make up Tax Law, even though he or she cannot litigate in court, but is able to dialogue with a jurist and steer the actions of his or her company or clients down the right path to strengthen the company through good tax management. It is good to understand, therefore, how tax laws are created, which so greatly affect the national economic system. (BARBOZA, 2017, p. 11)

Following this line of reasoning, the Brazilian legal system contains a series of taxes, regulated by the National Tax Code (*Código Tributário Nacional*). Its Article 3 defines what a tax is: “any compulsory pecuniary payment, in currency or whose value can be expressed in currency, that does not constitute a sanction for an unlawful act, is instituted by law and is collected through fully binding administrative activity” (BRASIL, 1966). In turn, Article 5 establishes the types of taxes, dividing them into taxes (*impostos*), fees (*taxas*) and betterment contributions (*contribuições de melhoria*) (BRASIL, 1966).

But for the tax planning of Brazilian companies, the type of tax that matters most is the tax proper (*imposto*), since fees and betterment contributions have little influence on a company’s profitability.

> For tax planning in companies, the most considered tax is the TAX (IMPOSTO), since Fees and Betterment Contributions are not relevant for this purpose. Among TAXES, we find a type not defined by the National Tax Code but instituted by the Federal Constitution, which gives it legitimacy. These are the social contributions. The Legal Nature of taxes must be observed for this purpose. (BARBOZA, 2017, p. 15)

Then, among the taxes that exert great influence on a company’s profitability and that need treatment within tax planning, the following taxes and contributions stand out, which must, in theory, be paid by the company in the course of its activity:

- a) IPI: Tax on Industrialized Products;
- b) ICMS: Tax on the Circulation of Goods and Services; PIS: Social Integration Program;
- c) COFINS: Contribution for Social Security Financing;
- d) INSS: Employer and Employee Contribution to the National Social Security Institute;
- e) IRPJ: Corporate Income Tax;
- f) CSLL: Social Contribution on Net Profit;
- g) ISSQN: Tax on Services of Any Nature.

One must also remember the ITCMD, the Tax on Causa Mortis Transfers and Gifts, which is extremely relevant within family succession, since it is the tax levied on the transfer of the company within the succession, materialized in the probate. It is paid once, according to the rate established in each State of the federation (see Note 1 below).

Therefore, tax planning is of the utmost importance in the incorporation of a company, as well as in the succession procedure, since through it there will be greater profitability for the family business company.

## 5. The Succession Procedure in Family Businesses

For the family business to subsist over the years, it is necessary that, besides succession and tax planning, there be an optimized procedure for the transfer of assets, as well as of the administration of the family’s property and company.

> The succession process represents one of the most important moments for optimizing the continuity of the family business. If this moment does not produce the expected results, the effectiveness of the family business may be seriously compromised.
>
> Two types of succession process can be identified in family businesses:
>
> a) family succession; and
>
> b) professional succession. (OLIVEIRA, 1999, p. 24)

It should be noted that, within the succession procedure, there must be no subjective interests, such as, for example, appointing the firstborn of the founding partner as president of the family company without his or her being qualified for that role. This is because, without aptitude and qualification, the result of such an appointment may be catastrophic for the company, possibly even leading to its bankruptcy due to poor management.

> It is not claimed that natural heirs are incompetent, but that the analysis and evaluation of the succession process must be truly realistic. If the heir is truly competent or is in a position to become competent, all is well. Otherwise, one must skip straight to the situation of professional succession. Remember the saying “competent grandfather, noble father and poor son.” (OLIVEIRA, 1999, p. 24-25)

It should also be noted that, in the succession procedure, there are advantages and disadvantages, as in every type of business.

Oliveira (1999, p. 27-30) argues that the advantages are: continuity of family command in the company; a decision-making process with agility and a high degree of flexibility in implementing actions; having, in the succession, a person with an ownership interest in optimizing the company’s current and future results; the possibility of more extensive and intensive training; deeper knowledge of the successor executive; optimized compensation systems; greater power of command over the successor executive at the beginning of the process; and, above all, a stronger family spirit.

The disadvantages, on the other hand, are the disputes that may occur among family members regarding the position each will occupy within the company. There may be difficulty in dismissing the successor executive, in addition to difficulties in performing different roles, as, for example, the father who at the same time performs the roles of father and director of the company (OLIVEIRA, 1999, p. 27-30).

Therefore, the succession procedure, as stated before, is closely linked to succession planning as well as to tax planning, since all of these institutions serve to optimize and sustain family businesses, as well as the circulation of profits in the country. Attention must be paid, however, to the disadvantages that exist in this type of procedure.

## 6. Asset Holding Companies as a Solution to Conflicts in the Succession Procedure

When speaking of tax planning and family succession, as discussed, great caution is needed regarding the administration of the family business and how it will take place, since many companies tend toward bankruptcy when they enter a succession process precisely for lack of planning, in every sense.

This bankruptcy, in most cases, is due to the conflict of subjective interests arising from the transition of business administration, as well as from those that may occur in the probate process.

For such conflicts of interest to be overcome and the family business to subsist, all members of the family must be seen as shareholders and not as family members. And as such, they must behave.

One way of promoting this way of seeing things, and thus a possible solution to such conflicts, is the figure of the holding company. Holdings are companies whose basic purpose is to hold shares of other companies; the expression comes from the English verb “hold,” meaning to keep, control or guard (OLIVEIRA, 1995, p. 19). That is, the purpose of the holding is to manage another company within a given situation, planning the transfer of its administration in succession *causa mortis*, as well as its tax administration.

In this vein, a holding company is not about producing profits and products, but about administering the profits produced or other companies.

> The holding must have a participation in the capital of other associated companies (investees) in sufficient quantity and quality to influence their administration. In general, the objectives of a holding company are:
>
> - to safeguard the interests of its shareholders, through interaction in various companies and businesses;
> - to act as principal shareholder of the affiliated companies, and may even have administrative management of the businesses;
> - to administer the investment portfolio of the business group;
> - to provide centralized services to the group’s companies, acting, in this case, as the embryo of a corporate administration; and
> - to represent the business group in a structured and homogeneous way, mainly through the consolidation of a set of administrative policies. (OLIVEIRA, 1995, p. 19)

With this, the idea is that the formation of holding companies facilitates the family succession procedure and its planning, allowing the holder of the assets, such as the founder of the family business, to determine the correct administration of his or her assets while still alive, for after his or her death, avoiding the collapse of the company and the dissipation of the assets he or she built.

Regarding holding companies, there are four main types to be defined (OLIVEIRA, 1995, p. 27). The pure holding is the one used by large groups and centralizes in a simple way the shareholding, even if minimal, in other companies.

In turn, the operating holding is the one that carries out operating activities, such as the production and sale of products. In between, there is the mixed holding, which carries out operating activities and also provides services, mainly to its affiliates.

Finally, there is the hybrid holding, used mostly in specific cases, such as situations of operational or tax structuring of the company.

Be that as it may, it should be noted that the types of holding vary according to the greatest profitability for the company, as well as the profile of each entrepreneur, since not all are equal, much less do they have the same market vision.

Thus, and within each profile, holding companies aim to optimize succession, facilitating “the planning, organization, control, as well as the directive process of the affiliated companies” (OLIVEIRA, 1995, p. 147).

Moreover, “in the case of holdings formed by family companies, it also provides their owners with a better distribution in life of their assets to their heirs, without being deprived of the effective and broad administrative process” (OLIVEIRA, 1995, p. 147).

Therefore, the main mission of a family company that uses holding companies for the administration of its profits, as well as for planning all the taxation that will be levied on it, is nothing more than organization, so that the company endures over the years, passing without major problems through succession *causa mortis* and the tax burden that applies.

## Concluding Remarks

Succession *causa mortis* can by itself become complicated, notably when there are conflicts of interest among heirs. The gravity of these conflicts can be such that, when there is a family business in the succession, the risk that it will not survive is high. This is without disregarding the high tax burden, which already demands careful planning.

In this scenario, the creation of holding companies presents itself as a means of protecting the family’s assets, given the aforementioned existence of conflicts of subjective interests regarding the administration of those assets.

Even more so because, when one speaks of succession law, the probate process immediately comes to mind, a procedure that is costly and wearing for the family as a whole, mainly within agribusiness, since the large companies in this sector are predominantly family-owned.

With regard to tax planning within succession, the creation of holding companies facilitates the administration of assets, given that the impact of the amount of taxes and charges owed to the State decreases significantly.

Therefore, so that the subjective interests of the members of the family company do not have influence, and, as a consequence, the family business does not collapse and the assets are not dissipated, the creation of the holding company presents itself as a possible solution, within the planning parameters presented. The interests will be administered by the holding, aiming at the financial health of the company, and the members of the family will be treated as partners of a limited-liability business company, each receiving his or her remuneration through participation quotas, where applicable.
